# FORTY BUTTONS, ONE SENTENCE
### How a music controller was rebuilt into a machine that writes language on stage

**Estimated runtime:** ~10:16 (≈145 wpm, dry narration)
**Visual style:** 3D wireframe / isometric vector motion graphics
**Audio:** dry narration, no music (optional non-musical cue — see AUDIO SYSTEM)
**Pipeline:** NotebookLM-compatible scene blocks
**Palette:** locked — carbon (#111318), type-white (#FFF4E0), signal-amber (#FFB020), inference-magenta (#FF3CC8)
**Data convention:** SOLID amber = verified on the running system · DASHED magenta = designed but never tested against hardware
**Scene count:** 12 (+ end card)
**Narration word count:** ~1,489

---

## AUDIO SYSTEM (optional, non-musical)

Baseline is dry narration over silence. If a bed is wanted, use one non-musical cue: a **soft mechanical key-press** — the dead, unpitched thock of a rubber pad being struck, repeating at a slow irregular interval. Never melodic, never on a grid. It plays only under the SOLID amber material, because that is the material that has actually been struck in software. It **cuts to silence the moment the piece moves to the controller**, and stays silent through the entire final scene, because the physical instrument has never made a sound. The script reads clean without it.

## SCENE 01 — COLD OPEN: ONE PAD

**VISUAL:** Black. A single wireframe square floats in isometric space, drawn in carbon outline — one rubber pad, seen from above and slightly to the side. It fills with SOLID signal-amber and the word MISREADS resolves inside it in type-white. The camera pulls back: the pad is one cell in a grid of forty, five rows by eight columns, the other thirty-nine still dark carbon. One by one, three more pads light — one in the row above, one below, one below that — and as each lights, its word lifts off the grid and stacks in the air to the right, assembling a line of text.

**NARRATION:** This is a button. On any normal night, it would trigger a video clip. Here it does something else. It is a word. Above it sits another button holding a different word, and below it another, and the machine is arranged so that pressing three of them in any order produces a sentence. Forty buttons, forty words, one grammar. The performer is not cutting between video files. The performer is writing, in front of an audience, at the speed of a drum fill.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** RES REACT LEXICON · 40 PADS · 40 WORDS

**TRANSITION:** The assembled sentence rotates flat into the plane of the grid. Cut.

## SCENE 02 — THE COUNT

**VISUAL:** The grid stands upright, isometric, all forty cells now outlined in amber. Three rows detach and hang in space as three stacks of eight. Between them, wireframe threads draw every possible path from stack one to stack two to stack three — the threads multiply until the space between the stacks is a solid amber weave. A counter climbs in the corner: 8 · 64 · 512.

**NARRATION:** Eight choices of subject. Eight verbs. Eight objects. Every path through those three stacks is a grammatical sentence, and there are five hundred and twelve of them. Add the punctuation row and it becomes four thousand and ninety-six lines, played over eight different backgrounds. All of it fits in forty buttons, because the machine is not storing sentences. It is storing parts, and letting the performer's hands do the combining. Nothing has to be written in advance.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** 8 × 8 × 8 = 512 SENTENCES · 4,096 LINES · 8 GROUNDS

**TRANSITION:** The weave of threads collapses into a single line of text. Cut.

## SCENE 03 — THE GRID IS THE SENTENCE

**VISUAL:** The grid rotates to face us square on. Each of the five rows is labelled at its left edge, top to bottom: SUBJECT, VERB, OBJECT, MARK, FIELD. To the right, a second wireframe rectangle appears — the projected output — and the four lines of text inside it sit at exactly the same heights as their rows on the grid. Amber guide-lines run horizontally from each row to its line on screen. The two objects are visibly the same shape.

**NARRATION:** The five rows of pads are the five slots of a sentence, and they are stacked in the order you read them. The top row is the subject, and it draws at the top of the screen. The row beneath it is the verb, and it draws beneath the subject. Row order is layer order is reading order. This sounds obvious. It replaced an earlier layout on the same hardware where the main content sat on the third row down, for reasons that had made sense to the software and never once made sense to a hand in the dark. The rest of the surface follows the same logic. The five sliders are not layer opacity; they are how present each part of speech is, so pulling one down removes the verb and leaves the nouns standing. The five buttons down the right edge sit beside the rows they empty. Every control on the instrument is named in the instrument's own terms, not the software's.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** SUBJECT · VERB · OBJECT · MARK · FIELD — ROW ORDER = READING ORDER

**TRANSITION:** The guide-lines snap taut and pull the two rectangles together into one. Cut.

## SCENE 04 — MADE OF NOTHING

**VISUAL:** A conventional composition is drawn first: a wireframe grid whose cells are each tethered by a long amber cable to a heavy block labelled with a file size, the blocks stacked into a wall far below. The cables snap, one at a time, and the blocks fall away into black. What is left is the grid alone, weightless, and a single small cube beside it labelled 755 KB.

**NARRATION:** A composition like this normally contains video — gigabytes of rendered footage, one dependency per cell. Move a folder and the clips come up empty. This one contains no video at all. No image files, no plugins, nothing external. Every one of the forty cells is a text generator built into the software itself, and even the background layer is text — rows of repeating characters used as texture. The entire instrument is one file of about seven hundred and fifty kilobytes, most of which is the little preview pictures on the buttons. It is smaller than one second of video. There is nothing to relink and nothing to forget in the van.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** 0 VIDEO FILES · 0 PLUGINS · 0 DEPENDENCIES · 755 KB

**TRANSITION:** The small cube spins once and unfolds back into the forty-cell grid. Cut.

## SCENE 05 — THE VOCABULARY

**VISUAL:** Three columns of eight words each, floating in type-white, drawn as flat wireframe plates. The camera drifts down them. Then a hand-shaped wireframe pointer strikes three plates at random, and the chosen words slide together into a line. It does this four times, quickly, and every result is a complete sentence.

**NARRATION:** The words were chosen so that every combination lands. The archive misreads a borrowed name. Nobody rehearses the long night. Every machine forgives its own echo. A slow comet broadcasts nothing at all. There is no wrong chord in this instrument, because the vocabulary was built to remove the possibility of one. That is the actual design: a constraint tight enough that coherence is guaranteed, which frees the player to stop thinking about grammar and think only about timing. The register is deliberate — broadcast-era, science-fictional, machine-poetic, and never violent. And the columns are playable too. Firing a whole column drops a complete pre-composed line on screen in a single press, so the same grid works two ways: assembled word by word when there is time, or thrown down whole when there is not.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** THE ARCHIVE / MISREADS / A BORROWED NAME · NO WRONG CHORD

**TRANSITION:** The four sentences fall away like cards off a deck. Cut.

## SCENE 06 — THE LEGEND

**VISUAL:** The physical controller appears for the first time — and it is drawn entirely in DASHED inference-magenta, unlike everything before it. Its five rows glow in the five row colours. Beside it, in SOLID amber, the output rectangle shows the same five colours at the same heights. A dashed magenta line runs from the controller to the screen and does not quite reach; there is a visible gap where it should connect.

**NARRATION:** The colours are not decoration. The software sends colour instructions back to the controller, so each row of pads lights in the same colour its words appear in on screen. Amber row, amber verb. The hardware becomes a legend for the output, readable at a glance, in the dark, at speed. Notice, though, that the controller is drawn differently from everything else in this piece. Hold that. We will come back to why.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** PAD COLOUR = WORD COLOUR · 98 SHORTCUTS · EVERY CONTROL ASSIGNED

**TRANSITION:** The camera pushes past the dashed controller and back into the solid amber screen. Cut.

## SCENE 07 — EVERY PAD BRINGS ITS OWN KNOBS

**VISUAL:** One pad lifts out of the grid. Eight small wireframe dials unfold from beneath it like landing gear, numbered one to eight. A second pad is struck; the first set of dials folds away and an identical set unfolds under the new pad. Then the camera holds on dial one as it turns, and the single word in the output rectangle swells — past the frame edge, past the other three lines, until one word is the entire screen and the other lines are still crisp underneath it.

**NARRATION:** Eight rotary controls are wired not to the software in general but to whichever pad was pressed last. Press a word, and the knobs become that word's knobs. Forty pads, eight dials each — three hundred and twenty individual connections, all written into the file directly. The first dial is size, and it runs from one half to ten, with the resting value sitting at roughly five percent of the knob's travel. A small turn takes one word from a line of text to the whole frame. That sensitivity is not a flaw that was tolerated. It is the instrument's loudest gesture, and it was left exactly where it fell. One dial was refused. Vertical position looks like the obvious thing to put on a knob, until you measure it — the underlying range runs to plus and minus thirty-two thousand, which means the entire useful travel occupies under two percent of the rotation. It would be unplayable. Every range was read off the running system before a knob was assigned to it, rather than after, and that one was rejected on the spot.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** 40 PADS × 8 DIALS = 320 LINKS · SIZE 0.5 → 10

**TRANSITION:** The oversized word contracts to a point and drops back into its pad. Cut.

## SCENE 08 — WHAT DOES NOT MOVE

**VISUAL:** A waveform enters from the left as an amber wireframe ribbon and splits into three bands. The lowest band threads down to the bottom row of the grid; the highest band threads to the punctuation row. Those two rows begin to pulse — the bottom slowly, the top sharply. The three word rows in the middle are traced with a thin type-white outline and do not move at all. A small dashed magenta bracket sits beside the still rows.

**NARRATION:** Two of the five rows listen to the music. The background texture is driven by the bass and breathes on the kick drum. The punctuation is driven by the treble and snaps on the hi-hat. The three rows that carry the actual words are deliberately excluded, and never move. This is the central decision of the whole audio design. An instrument whose entire subject is language has to keep the language readable. Making the words pulse would look extraordinary for about ten seconds and be illegible for the rest of the set. The motion was pushed to the two elements that carry no meaning. Even the decay times are chosen to read rather than to impress: eight hundred milliseconds on the bass, which the eye reads as breathing, and two hundred on the treble, which reads as a snap. And both rows rest at a deliberately high floor, so that if no audio is connected at all — a wrong cable, a different venue, a laptop on a table — nothing disappears. The instrument simply stops moving and stays completely readable.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** FIELD = BASS · MARK = HIGHS · WORDS = STILL

**TRANSITION:** The waveform ribbon flattens into a straight line and becomes the edge of a document. Cut.

## SCENE 09 — WRITE THE FILE, NOT THE APP

**VISUAL:** Split isometric space. On the left, an application is drawn as a tall glass block, with hundreds of thin amber darts flying into it one after another; the block stutters, and a few darts bounce off. On the right, the same composition is drawn as a flat sheet of structured text, and a single amber beam sweeps across it once, top to bottom, leaving a completed grid behind. A stopwatch beside the right panel reads four seconds.

**NARRATION:** There are two ways to build something like this. The normal way is to drive the running application through its programming interface — create the composition, add the layers, load each cell, set each parameter. Hundreds of separate instructions into live software, which on this machine has a documented history of timing out, crashing, and occasionally reporting success for things that did not happen. The other way is to notice that the composition file is structured text. So the file was written directly, offline, in about four seconds, and only then opened. It cannot crash the application, because the application is not involved.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** BUILD THE FILE OFFLINE · VALIDATE · THEN OPEN IT ONCE

**TRANSITION:** The right-hand sheet folds into a cube and the camera enters it. Cut.

## SCENE 10 — THREE THINGS THAT WERE WRONG

**VISUAL:** Inside the cube: three wireframe panels in a row, each SOLID amber, each stamped with a label. As the narration reaches each one, that panel inverts — the solid amber lines break into DASHED magenta and the panel flips over to show a corrected version behind it. Panel one: a sentence stack, upside down, righting itself. Panel two: six blank squares among forty. Panel three: a small dial detaching from a pad and re-attaching to the wrong object one level above it.

**NARRATION:** Three pieces of established knowledge turned out to be wrong, and all three were caught the same way — by looking at the real output instead of trusting the note. First, the vertical axis runs the opposite direction to the mathematical convention, so the first build rendered every sentence upside down. Second, the software generated blank preview pictures for six of the forty buttons — the ones holding thin marks like a period or a question mark — and the function offered for replacing them displayed correctly while silently saving nothing. That format had to be decoded and the pictures written into the file by hand. Third, the connection that gives each pad its knobs attaches, through the interface, to the wrong object entirely. None of these are in any manual. Each one cost a rebuild.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** AXIS INVERTED · 6 OF 40 BLANK · DIAL ON THE WRONG OBJECT

**TRANSITION:** The three panels stack into one and go dark. Cut.

## SCENE 11 — THE OPEN LOOP

**VISUAL:** The full system is drawn one last time, and now the difference is unmistakable. The screen, the composition, the layers, the words — all SOLID amber. The controller — every pad, every knob, every fader — entirely DASHED magenta. The signal path runs from software to hardware and stops at a visible gap. No hand appears anywhere in the frame. [AUDIO: silence.]

**NARRATION:** Here is what has actually been proven. The composition opens. The sentence assembles and reads correctly. The colours render as specified. The three hundred and twenty dial connections load and demonstrably change the picture. All forty button images are distinct and stored inside the file. Every one of the sixty-five existing compositions on that machine was verified untouched, byte for byte. And here is what has not been proven. No pad has ever been physically pressed. No light has been confirmed on the hardware. No knob has been turned by a hand. The entire controller layout — all ninety-eight assignments — is designed and written, not played. That gap is why the controller has been drawn dashed since the moment it first appeared, and it is stated in three separate places in the project, in the same words, so that nobody discovers it on stage.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** PLAYED IN SOFTWARE · NEVER TOUCHED BY HARDWARE

**TRANSITION:** The dashed controller holds alone in frame for two full seconds. Cut.

---

## SCENE 12 — CLOSE

**VISUAL:** The forty-cell grid returns, empty and carbon. A plain list of words scrolls past it in type-white and each word drops into a cell until the grid is full. The list scrolls again — different words, another language, then a single unfamiliar poem — and each time the grid refills. The camera rises until the grid is one small amber rectangle in black.

**NARRATION:** The instrument that exists today holds one vocabulary, forty words long, chosen for a particular register. But the words are not really in the instrument. They are in a list, in a script, and running that script rebuilds the whole thing in four seconds. Change the list and the machine is a different machine — another language, a specific show, a commissioned text, somebody else's poem. The composition was the deliverable. The generator is the actual object. Forty buttons, and the only thing that decides what they say is what somebody types into a list.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:** THE COMPOSITION IS THE OUTPUT · THE GENERATOR IS THE INSTRUMENT

**TRANSITION:** The rectangle narrows to a single lit pad, then to black. Hold 3 seconds.

**Production notes:**
- The dashed convention is doing double duty here and it is the reason the piece works: it marks inference, and it also carries the project's single honest caveat. The controller must be dashed from its first appearance in Scene 06, with no explanation, so that Scene 11 pays it off. Do not explain it early.
- Scene 07 is the strongest visual beat — one word swelling to fill the frame while the other lines stay crisp. Hold it two beats longer than feels comfortable.
- Scene 11 is the real ending. Scene 12 is a coda; if runtime is tight, trim Scene 05 (The Vocabulary) rather than either of them, and let the sample sentences in Scene 01 carry that work.
- Scenes 09 and 10 can collapse into one if the piece needs to come in under nine minutes, but the "three things were wrong" beat is the most transferable material in the script and is worth protecting.
- If the audio cue is used, the key-press thock must stop at Scene 06 and never return. The final scene is silent because the instrument is.
